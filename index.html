The idea, in 30 seconds

  • Public by default is the problem. On a normal blockchain anyone can read balances, counterparties and history forever. That works for open markets and badly for real life.
  • Midnight splits the ledger. Midnight keeps a public state for what must be verifiable and a private state for what must stay yours, using zero-knowledge proofs — mostly generated on your own device.
  • Selective disclosure is the product. Prove “over 18”, “paid in full” or “this message is from me” without revealing the birth date, the amount, or the rest of the conversation.
  • NIGHT and DUST. NIGHT is Midnight’s token, held on Cardano; holding NIGHT generates DUST, the renewable resource that pays for shielded Midnight transactions.

11 · Make it unguessable — secret strength & salt for commitments

Tool 8's honest limit, made measurable: anyone holding your commitment hash can guess at it offline, at machine speed, with nobody watching — so a commitment is only as safe as the secret behind it. 12 · Prove you're on the list — without showing the list

An allowlist, a voter roll, a set of eligible wallets: publish one short hash — the root — and the whole list is committed. Later, any one member can prove they're on it with a handful of sibling hashes, and a verifier who knows the root can check that proof without ever seeing the rest of the list. That structure is a Merkle tree, and the real SHA-256 for it runs locally here. Honestly labelled limits: a Merkle proof is not zero-knowledge — it reveals the entry itself, its position, the sibling hashes and (from the proof's length) the rough size of the list. The other entries stay behind their hashes, but a guessable entry can be checked by hashing a guess (tool 11's lesson), so real lists salt low-entropy entries. An odd entry at the end of a level is promoted unchanged, never duplicated. This page's hashed-message format is its own teaching format, not a specific chain's tree format — and nothing here connects to a chain or a wallet.

13 · Split a secret — no single share reveals it

Some secrets should not exist in just one place. Split a secret here and it is XORed with fresh random bytes generated locally, producing shares with a stark property: any share on its own is random bytes and reveals nothing about the secret's content, but all of them together rebuild it exactly. That is real secret sharing computed locally, not a simulation — the same one-time-pad maths that makes each incomplete set useless to whoever holds it. Honestly labelled limits: this is all-of-n sharing — every share is required, so losing one share loses the secret forever; real systems that need recovery use threshold (k-of-n) sharing such as Shamir's, where any k of n shares suffice — that scheme is named here, not implemented by this tool (tool 15 below implements it). A share is exactly as long as the secret, so a share's length leaks the secret's length. 14 · Spend it once, stay private — notes & nullifiers

How does a shielded ledger stop you spending the same private coin twice, if it cannot see the coin? With two hashes of the note's secret, made for two different jobs. Creating a note publishes only its commitment hash. Spending it publishes a different hash — its nullifier — and the ledger's one mechanical rule is that a nullifier may appear only once, ever. Try it: create a note, spend it, then try to spend it again. This is a simplified teaching model of the note-and-nullifier idea, built from this page's own SHA-256 formats — it is not how a real Midnight note is constructed: real notes use random secrets and keys nobody can guess, plus a zero-knowledge proof that the note exists and the spender owns it. A human-chosen secret here can be dictionary-checked against both hashes (tool 11's lesson), and if it ever leaks, its commitment and nullifier become linkable after the fact. 15 · Split with a safety net — any k of n shares (Shamir)

Tool 13's split has a hard edge: lose one share and the secret is gone. This is the threshold scheme it named — Shamir's secret sharing, computed locally for real. Each byte of your secret becomes the constant term of a polynomial over GF(256) whose other coefficients are fresh random bytes, and each share is that polynomial evaluated at a different point. Any k of the n shares rebuild the secret exactly by interpolation; k−1 shares are consistent with every possible secret, so they reveal nothing about its content. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited library — and there is no checksum, so with exactly k shares, one wrong or mistyped share rebuilds a wrong secret silently. A share is exactly as long as the secret, so a share's length leaks the secret's length. 16 · Seal it so only they can read it — password-encrypted messages

A hash (tools 8, 12, 14) is one-way — nobody can un-hash it. Encryption is the opposite tool: reversible, but only for whoever holds the key. Seal a message here and it is encrypted for real, locally on this device, with AES-GCM: your password is stretched by PBKDF2 (210,000 rounds, a fresh random salt per seal) into the key, and the sealed text — safe to publish on its own — carries only the salt, the IV and the ciphertext. AES-GCM is authenticated, so a wrong password or one changed character fails to open instead of returning gibberish. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited encryption product, and its safety is exactly your password's safety — PBKDF2 slows offline guessing but cannot save a weak password (tool 11 measures that), and the sealed text's length reveals the message's approximate length. Send the password by a different channel from the sealed text: anyone with both can read it. 17 · Sign it — prove it came from you, without sharing your key

Encryption (tool 16) hides a message; a signature does the opposite job: the message stays public, but anyone can check that whoever holds one private key endorsed exactly these words — and that not one character changed since. This makes a real ECDSA key pair locally on this device, on the P-256 curve (the curve behind passkeys and WebAuthn; Bitcoin and Ethereum use its sibling curve secp256k1, which Web Crypto does not offer) and signs with ECDSA over SHA-256. The public key is safe to publish — it is how people recognise “you”. The private key IS the identity: anyone holding it can sign as you, with no recovery and no undo, so guard it exactly like a seed phrase. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited wallet; a signature does not hide the message, does not prove a legal name or real-world identity, and does not prove when the signing happened. Keys exist only in this page — nothing is stored or sent, and reloading loses the private key unless you saved it. 18 · Agree on a secret nobody saw — key agreement

Sealing (tool 16) needs a password you already share, and signing (tool 17) proves who holds a key — but how do two people who have never met end up with a secret they both know and nobody else does, over a channel everyone can read? With key agreement — real ECDH on the P-256 curve, computed locally here: each side makes a key pair, the public keys are the only things exchanged, and each side mixes its own private key with the other's public key. Both sides land on exactly the same 32-byte shared secret, even though that secret is never exchanged and cannot be worked out from the two public keys alone. That handshake is what sits underneath private messaging. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Real systems never use the raw shared secret directly as a key — they run it through a key-derivation function first — and agreement alone does not prove who the other public key belongs to: a man-in-the-middle who swaps both public keys completes two separate agreements and can read everything, which is why real messengers let you compare a fingerprint of the secret, never the secret itself, over a channel you already trust. The secret is displayed on this page only so you can practise both sides in one place; a real app never shows it. Never paste a real wallet's private key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys.

19 · Stretch one secret into proper keys — key derivation (HKDF)

Tool 18 ends on a warning: real systems never use the raw shared secret directly as a key. This is the step that warning points at. Key derivation takes one secret and stretches it into separate, independent keys — one secret, many keys — but never the secret itself as a key. It runs locally for real here with HKDF (RFC 5869) over SHA-256: the purpose you pick is mixed into the derivation, so a different purpose means a different key — the messaging key, the storage key and the backup key from one secret share nothing usable, and leaking one reveals nothing about the others or the secret. An optional salt (tool 11's generator makes them) separates sessions: same secret, same purpose, different salt, different key. The salt is not a secret — both sides must pick the same purpose and the same salt, and they may exchange the salt openly. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited key-management product, and derivation does not strengthen a weak input — a guessable secret derives guessable keys, because anyone can run the same public derivation; HKDF assumes its input is already high-entropy key material, like tool 18's output, which is why there is deliberately no password stretching here. 20 · Use the key — lock a message with the key you both derived

Tools 18 and 19 end with a key nobody has used yet — this is where it gets used. (Tool 16 is the contrast: it starts from a human password, so it must stretch it first, and its sealed text carries a salt. Here the sealed text carries only a fresh random IV per message.) Locking and opening run locally for real, and the authentication tag means a wrong key fails outright, exactly like a tampered or truncated message — there is no password fallback and no best guess. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app — real messengers add ratcheting, message numbering and key erasure on top of this shape. The key itself is the whole secret: whoever it leaks to reads everything locked with it, past and future, until both sides derive a fresh one, and the locked text still leaks the message's approximate length. 21 · Know it's really from them — sign it, then seal it

Tool 20's seal has a gap worth naming plainly: AES-GCM proves the key, not the person. Anyone who holds the session key can seal a message in anyone's name, and whoever opens it cannot tell — the seal checks out, the words look right, and the sender is whoever the opener assumes. This tool closes the gap the way real messengers do: the signature goes inside the seal. The sender signs the message with their tool-17 signing key first, then seals message and signature together with the session key. The opener opens the seal, then checks the signature against the sender's public key — and the two answers stay separate: a message can open perfectly and still fail the signature check, in which case the words are shown but flagged, because private is not the same as from them. Honestly labelled limits: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. The signature proves the signing key, not a legal name, and it only means anything if the public key really is the sender's — get it from them somewhere a swap would be visible, the tool-18 lesson. 22 · One key per message — the ratchet

Tool 20 names the price of one key for everything: whoever it leaks to reads everything locked with it, past and future. This tool builds the fix real messengers use — a ratchet — in its simplest symmetric form: one fresh key per message, and the key moves on the moment it has been used. A chain state is one line, p4a-chain-v1:<position>:<chain key>, starting from a tool-19 session key at position 0. Each step runs HKDF (tool 19's machinery) twice over the chain key with two different labels: one output is this message's own key, sealing through tool 20's exact format, and the other is the next chain key. HKDF runs one way only, so there is no way to recompute an earlier message key from a later state — overwrite an old state and its messages are gone for good, even to someone who later gets your current state. That is forward secrecy for the past. The honest limits are the other half of the lesson: this chain protects the past only — a leaked current state opens every message after it, until both sides agree fresh keys with tools 18–19 and start a new chain; messages open strictly in order, because this simple chain keeps no skipped-message keys — tool 25 below adds that store, with numbered seals; each direction needs its own chain, started from a different tool-19 purpose or salt; and a chain stops at a labelled cap of 1,000,000 messages rather than running unbounded. A failed open advances nothing, so an early message can be retried when its turn comes. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app — real messengers add a second, asymmetric ratchet on top, which is what heals a chain after a leak — tool 23 below builds a simplified version of that healing step, done by hand. 23 · Heal the chain — a fresh agreement restarts the ratchet

Tool 22's chain protects the past and says so plainly: a leaked current state opens every message after it, until both sides agree fresh keys. This tool is that recovery, done by hand — a simplified version of the second, asymmetric ratchet real messengers run automatically. Both holders of one direction's chain make brand-new agreement pairs with tool 18 and swap the public keys openly — public keys need no secrecy, though they still need to really be each other's (tool 18's fingerprint check). Each side mixes the fresh shared secret — which only the two new private keys can produce — with the chain key they both already hold: the secret goes into HKDF as the input, the current chain key as the salt, under its own label. The result is a new chain at position 0 that someone holding only the leaked state cannot follow them to: they have the old chain key, but without a new private key they cannot compute the fresh secret, and the healed key exists nowhere until both ingredients meet. The honest limits: healing protects only what comes after it — it does not unlock the past, reopen deleted messages, or help while a leak is still live on a compromised device; both sides must heal from exactly the same current state, or their healed chains differ and seals simply fail; and one manual heal is a teaching simplification of a step real apps do on every reply. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Never paste a real wallet key or a production session key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys from tools 18–19.

24 · Hide the length — pad it to one size before sealing

Every seal on this page is honest about the same leak — tools 13, 15, 16 and 20 all name it: a sealed text's length tracks the message's length, so a relay, a ledger watcher or a curious server learns roughly how much was said without reading a word. A one-word answer, a four-figure amount and a long confession seal to visibly different sizes: the length itself is the leak. The standard fix is padding, and that is what this tool does for real: the message is padded up to a fixed bucket size before it is locked — an 8-byte header (a "P4AP" marker plus the message's true byte length) goes in front of its UTF-8 bytes, fresh random bytes fill the rest, and the whole block, exactly 256, 1,024, 4,096 or 16,384 bytes, is locked with AES-GCM under a tool-19 session key, in its own p4a-paddedseal-v1 format. The padding sits inside the lock, so the authentication tag covers the filler too: changing any of it fails outright. Two messages in the same bucket produce locked lines of exactly the same length; the opener reads the true length back from the header and the filler is never shown. The honest limits: padding hides length only inside a bucket — which bucket a message landed in still shows; timing and frequency are untouched, and a watcher who sees many messages can still learn from the pattern; and real messengers pad inside a ratcheted protocol with cover traffic on top. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Never paste a real wallet key or a production session key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys from tools 18–19.

25 · Out of order, still private — skipped message keys

Tool 22's ratchet is honest about its limit: it opens messages strictly in order, because it keeps no store for skipped message keys. Real networks do not deliver in order — a message is delayed, retried or routed another way — and real messengers answer with a skipped-key store, the way Signal's protocol does. This tool builds it. The seal carries its position on the outside, p4a-oooseal-v1:<position>:<iv>:<cipher> — tool 20's exact AES-GCM seal under that position's ratchet key, numbered, so the receiver knows which key it needs. When the message for position 5 arrives while your chain sits at 3, the chain steps forward, the keys for 3 and 4 go into a small store (p4a-skipped-v1), and 5 opens now; when 3 and 4 finally arrive, their stored keys open them — and each stored key is erased the moment it is used, so a late message opens exactly once and a replay finds no key and a chain that has already passed it. The store is bounded on purpose: at most 32 keys kept, and one open will not step the chain more positions than the store could hold the skipped keys for — warehousing keys for messages that may never arrive is the denial-of-service shape real protocols cap for the same reason, and a refusal consumes nothing, so the message can be retried once the gap closes honestly. The honest limits: a stored key is a past message's whole secret kept alive past its turn — forward secrecy for those positions is paused, not held, until the late message arrives and the key is erased, and someone who copies the store reads exactly the messages it holds keys for; the position on the outside is metadata a watcher can see, like tool 24's bucket; and this is still the symmetric chain, so tool 23's heal remains the answer to a leak. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Never paste a real wallet key or a production session key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys from tools 18–19.

26 · For their key only — a sealed box anyone can close, only they can open

Every seal before this one needs something shared in advance: tool 16 a password both sides know, tools 20–25 a session key both sides derived together in tool 18's live, two-sided agreement. This tool serves the most ordinary case those leave out — sending something private to someone who is not there. They published a public key, and that is all you have. It is the sealed-box pattern behind “encrypt to their public key”: anyone who knows your public key can close a box for you, and only the matching private key opens it — no handshake and no shared password. Closing a box makes a fresh, one-message agreement pair, mixes it with the recipient's public key (tool 18), stretches the result once with HKDF — the one-message public key itself as the salt, so this box's key is bound to this box alone — and seals with tool 20's AES-GCM, in one versioned line: p4a-box-v1:<ephemeral public key>:<iv>:<cipher>. The ephemeral public key rides on the outside in plain view because it is not a secret: the recipient mixes it with their private key, lands on the same secret, re-derives the same key and opens; anyone else lands on a different secret and the seal refuses. The one-message private key is never stored, shown or sent — once the box is closed it has done its only job. The honest limits: a box proves nothing about who sent it — the recipient's public key is public, so anyone can close a box in anyone's name, and tool 21's signature inside a seal is this hub's answer when the sender matters; there is no ratchet here, so if your private key is copied later, every box ever closed to it opens — forward secrecy belongs to tools 22–25, whose keys are erased as they are used; and a public key only protects you if it really is theirs, because a swapped key closes every box to the swapper instead. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Never paste a real wallet key or a production private key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys from tool 18.

27 · A box that names its sender — sign it, then close it in the box

Tool 26's sealed box serves the person who is not there, and names its own limit in the same breath: a box proves nothing about who sent it, because the recipient's public key is public and anyone can close a box in anyone's name. Tool 21 is this hub's answer when the sender matters — a signature inside a seal — but it needs a session key both sides derived together, which is exactly what the box case does not have. This tool puts the two together: sign it, then close it in the box. The sender signs the message with their signing key (tool 17), and the signature rides inside the box in the same signed envelope tool 21 uses — inside, so only the recipient ever sees it, and a watcher cannot even tell this box is signed. The envelope is then closed exactly like tool 26's box — a fresh, one-message agreement pair (tool 18), HKDF stretched once, AES-GCM (tool 20) — with two deliberate separations: the stretching step uses its own label, so signed and plain boxes derive unrelated keys from the same secret, and the line gets its own format, p4a-authbox-v1:<ephemeral public key>:<iv>:<cipher>, so neither box can be relabelled into the other — a relabelled line fails closed at the seal before any envelope is read. Opening keeps tool 21's honest split: opening and verifying are two separate answers, so the box can open perfectly while the name does not check out — that result is shown with its message, not hidden. The honest limits are inherited from both parents: the signature proves the signing key, not a legal name; both public keys have to really be theirs — a swapped recipient key hands every box to the swapper, and a swapped sender key makes a stranger's signature verify under the wrong name; there is still no ratchet, so if the recipient's private key is copied later, every signed box ever closed to it opens, signatures and all; and a copied box line opens again — nothing inside proves when it was signed or that it was sent only once, so freshness is the application's job, not the box's. Honestly labelled: this is a teaching implementation, not an audited messaging app. Never paste a real wallet key or a production private key into any web page, including this one — practise with throwaway keys from tools 17 and 18.

28 · Is that really their key? — the safety number

Every tool from 17 on ends on the same warning: a signature, a sealed box, an agreement — all of them prove the key, and the key only helps if the public key you hold is really theirs. The swap that defeats the whole suite happens before any message: a directory, a profile page or a person in the middle hands you a different public key, and from then on every check passes — against the swapper's key. This tool is the check that runs before trust: a safety number for a pair of public keys. Both keys are sorted and hashed together under a domain label, so both people compute exactly the same number from the same two published keys, in either order, without meeting and without any secret. The number is built from public keys alone, so it is safe to read aloud, print or post, and revealing it reveals nothing about any private key. Twelve groups of five digits — long enough that a swapper cannot realistically find a key that lands on the same number, short enough to compare over a phone call. 32 · Prove you know the key — a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge

Tool 5 above simulates the zero-knowledge pattern and says so in so many words — a teaching simulation, not a cryptographic proof. This tool runs a real one: the Schnorr identification protocol, the classic proof of knowledge that modern zero-knowledge systems grow from, computed locally on the same P-256 curve as tools 17, 18 and 30. Say you need to convince someone you hold the private key behind a published public key — without revealing the key, and without signing anything (tool 17's signatures bind a message and can be shown around forever; a proof answers one fresh question, once). It takes three moves, and the order is the security. First, the commitment must be fixed before the challenge is chosen: you pick a fresh secret nonce, publish only its public half as the commitment, and keep the nonce in a prover-only state line. Second, the verifier — only now — draws a fresh, unpredictable challenge number; a challenge of zero is refused, because with zero the answer is just the nonce and proves nothing about the key. Third, you answer with a single number blended from the nonce, the challenge and your private key, and the verifier checks one curve equation against your public key: it balances only if the answer was genuinely built from the private number, yet the answer itself reveals neither secret — one equation, two unknowns. The two ways it breaks are plain: a prover who sees the challenge first can work backwards and fake the whole proof for a key they do not hold, which is why the commitment coming first is the proof and not ceremony; and answering two challenges with the same commitment leaks the private key itself — two answers differ by a multiple of it that anyone can divide out — so one state answers exactly one challenge and is then thrown away. A completed proof is not a signature: it signs no message and authorises nothing, and it proves knowledge of the private key, not a legal name — the key could be anyone's, borrowed or stolen; what is proved is that the answerer holds it, against this challenge, now — an old answer replayed against a new challenge simply fails. 34 · One of us signed it — a ring signature

36 · Signed without seeing — a blind signature

37 · It takes a quorum — a threshold signature

Every signature on this page so far has had exactly one key behind it: one holder, one point of failure, one person who can be coerced, robbed or simply asleep when the group needs to act. 38 · No dealer ever saw it — distributed key generation

Tool 37 above ends on a named flaw: a dealer splits the group key, and the dealer sees the whole key at setup — one person who could keep a copy, be coerced, or simply be the wrong person to trust with everything. 39 · A signature that waits for a secret — adaptor signatures

Every signature on this page is finished the moment it is made: tool 33's line either verifies or it does not, and nothing that happens afterwards can change that. Some agreements need the opposite shape — a signature that is complete except for one missing number, so that revealing that number finishes the signature, and finishing the signature reveals the number. That is an adaptor signature, run here locally on tool 33's own Schnorr scheme. The missing number is an ordinary secret scalar t, the adaptor secret; what the world sees of it is only its point T = t×G, the adaptor point, which reveals nothing about t. The signer makes a pre-signature: draw a nonce, commit to it as usual at R, then shift the commitment by the adaptor point, R̂ = R + T, and draw tool 33's challenge over the shifted commitment and the message. The answer s′ is tool 32's arithmetic unchanged — but it answers for R, while the challenge was drawn over R̂, so the line (p4a-adaptor-v1) is not a signature: tool 33's verifier checks it and says no, because it sits exactly one adaptor point short of balancing. Anyone holding T can still check the pre-signature is genuine — s′×G must equal (R̂ − T) + e×Y — so both sides can confirm, before anything is revealed, that exactly one number is missing and it is the number whose point is T. Adapting is adding that number: s = s′ + t, and the result is one ordinary p4a-schnorr-v1 line that tool 33's verifier accepts. Extraction is the same addition read backwards: anyone holding both lines computes t = s − s′ and recovers the adaptor secret itself — that symmetry is the mechanism, not a side effect. Take a stranger's pre-signature locked to a secret's point, and the moment either side publishes an adapted signature the other side can extract the secret and adapt their own — which is why real systems build atomic swaps and payment locks out of exactly this shape: two signatures on two chains, one secret, both complete or neither does. The honest limits are plain. The pre-signature is worthless until adapted — it verifies as nothing and spends nothing; it proves only that its maker knew the signing key and aimed it at this adaptor point. The adaptor point has to really be the point of the secret the agreement is about: a swapped point locks the signature to a stranger's secret, and adapting with any other number produces a line tool 33's verifier rejects — check the point the way tool 28 checks a key, over a channel you already trust. Publication is the reveal: an adapted signature shown to anyone who holds the pre-signature hands them the secret, so there is no adapting privately to one verifier — adapt for one holder of the pre-signature and you have adapted for all of them. 40 · Many keys, one signature — aggregate signatures

Tools 37 and 38 split one key across a quorum, so any t of n holders can sign. 42 · Hide the amount, keep the maths — Pedersen commitments

Tool 8's hash commitment can prove a value was not changed, but it can do no arithmetic: a hash of 750 and a hash of 250 cannot be combined into anything checkable against 1000. 46 · Pick one, learn one — oblivious transfer

50 · Fetch one, tell neither — private information retrieval

Every tool above hides data, a message, or a question about a set. This one hides a lookup. A small catalogue — a directory, a price list, a table of notes — sits on servers anyone can ask, and you want exactly one row of it. Asking in plain words gives the row away to whoever is asked: the question itself is the private fact, and encrypting the answer changes nothing, because the server had to read the question to answer it. This tool is the two-server shape of private information retrieval, and its arithmetic is the plainest on this page: XOR. In the first form you name only how many rows the catalogue holds and which one you want, and the page draws a fresh coin flip for every row — a random mask, one bit per row. The first server is asked for the XOR of the rows where the mask has a 1; the second server is asked the same question with exactly one bit flipped, the wanted row's bit. Neither query names your row. A mask is uniform random bits, so a server holding one query holds a pattern that could have been drawn for any wanted row at all — the very same first query could pair with a second asking for any other row — and that is information-theoretic, not a hardness assumption: there is nothing to crack, because your row is not in the query in any form. Each server, in the second form, XORs the rows its query selects — every row first padded into a block of one common length, a two-byte length header in front, so rows of different lengths can be XORed at all, and so no row's true length ever shows — and returns one block. In the third form you XOR the two answers: every row the mask selected was folded in twice and cancels, the wanted row was folded in once, by the second server alone, and stands alone in the result; its header is read, its bytes taken, and the padding behind them must be exactly zero, or the finish fails closed rather than decoding a wrong row. What each side learns is stated plainly. Each server learns the size of the catalogue it holds and the length of its longest row — every answer is exactly one padded block — and nothing about which row you wanted, from its own query alone. You learn your row, and the padded block length, and no other row. The honest limits are plain, and the first is load-bearing: the two servers must not compare queries. The XOR of the two masks is a single 1 standing exactly on your row, so two servers that collude — or one operator quietly running both — learn the wanted row instantly and completely. This shape buys its privacy with non-collusion; where that cannot be had, real systems turn to single-server schemes built on lattice assumptions, which this page does not pretend to offer. An answer is not authenticated either: a dishonest server corrupts the result, and while a broken length header or non-zero padding is caught at the finish, a carefully wrong answer could in principle decode as a different plausible row — you can catch nonsense, not prove which server lied. The catalogue itself is not hidden and is not meant to be: the servers hold it in the clear, its contents are the public part, and only your choice is private.
